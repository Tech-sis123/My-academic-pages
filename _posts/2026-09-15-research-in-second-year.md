I published five papers in my second year of university. I'm writing this because I've been asked how several times, and the honest answer is not the tidy narrative people seem to want.

## The honest version

I did not plan to have five papers. I wrote one because a course required a research paper, and I found the topic genuinely interesting, so I wrote a real one instead of a minimal one. That paper went to a venue. When it was accepted I saw that the process was less opaque than I'd imagined.

The structural insight is this: at most Nigerian universities, you are already doing work in your courses that is publishable if you treat it like research rather than coursework. The difference is mostly framing and effort level. A well-scoped problem, a real method, real results, a proper literature review. The work isn't fundamentally harder than good coursework. It just has to be oriented toward a contribution rather than toward a grade.

## What actually makes a paper publishable

Three things:

**A problem with a clear boundary.** The worst papers try to solve too much. Your second-year paper should have a tight scope — one problem, one method, one honest evaluation. "A machine learning approach to X" is publishable. "A survey of all ML approaches to everything in the domain of X" is usually not, unless you're very good at surveys.

**A method that matches the claim.** If you're claiming your system performs better than a baseline, you need a real baseline comparison. If you're claiming a framework is practical, you need to have implemented it and tested it. The mismatch between what papers claim and what they actually demonstrate is the most common reason papers get rejected.

**An honest evaluation.** Reviewers can tell when you've only reported results that make your method look good. Include the cases where it doesn't work. A paper that says "our approach outperforms X except in Y condition because Z" is more credible than one that only shows the wins.

## What venue to target

Start with venues you can actually get to. For engineering students in Nigeria: SPE STSE for petroleum or signal processing applications, IEEE student paper competitions, local conferences that have proceedings. These are not lesser venues. The skill you're building is how to take a problem from idea to a written paper with claims you can defend. That skill doesn't require Nature to develop.

## On working alone vs. with others

I've done both. Working alone means you move faster and the ideas are entirely yours. Working with collaborators means you see the problem from more angles, and the paper is usually better for it. My collaboration with researchers from St. Petersburg Polytechnic on the fair mining deal paper taught me that cross-institutional work forces you to make your assumptions explicit in ways that solo work doesn't.

## What it doesn't mean

Publishing five papers as an undergrad doesn't mean you're a researcher yet. It means you've learned how to write in a form that other people in the field can engage with. That's a skill. It's not the same as having done work that significantly changes how the field thinks about something.

I'm trying to do the latter. I don't know yet whether I'm succeeding. But the former — the ability to write clearly about a scoped problem — is learnable, and learning it early is worth it.
